NMN and Fertility: What Research Says About Egg Quality and NAD+

Can NMN support fertility? A review of animal and early human research on NAD+ levels, egg quality, ovarian aging and male fertility markers.

Fertility clinics have started paying attention to NAD+ biology, and for good reason. Egg cells are among the most energy-hungry cells in the human body, and their quality declines sharply with age. A growing body of research suggests NAD+ depletion is one of the mechanisms behind that decline. NMN, as a direct NAD+ precursor, is now being studied as a way to support reproductive aging. Here is what the evidence actually shows, and where the hype outruns the data.

Why NAD+ matters for egg quality

An oocyte (egg cell) contains more mitochondria than almost any other cell type, up to several hundred thousand per cell. Those mitochondria power everything from chromosome separation during maturation to the first days of embryo development. Mitochondria run on NAD+.

The problem: NAD+ levels in ovarian tissue fall with age, and this decline tracks closely with the drop in egg quality seen from the mid-30s onward. Poor NAD+ availability is linked to spindle defects during cell division, which is the main cause of chromosomal abnormalities in eggs from older ovaries.

The animal research

The most cited study is Bertoldo et al. (2020), published in Cell Reports. Researchers gave NMN to aged mice and found:

  • Restored NAD+ levels in oocytes of aged mice
  • Improved egg quality, spindle assembly and chromosome alignment
  • Higher fertilisation rates and better embryo development
  • Increased live birth rates in aged females

Follow-up rodent studies have replicated the core finding: boosting NAD+ in aged ovaries improves oocyte quality markers. On the male side, animal studies show NAD+ precursors can improve sperm motility and reduce oxidative damage in sperm cells, though this research is thinner.

What about human evidence?

This is where honesty matters: there are no published human trials showing NMN improves pregnancy or live birth rates. What we do have:

  • Human trials confirming oral NMN supplementation reliably raises blood NAD+ levels (Yoshino et al., 2021)
  • Human data showing NMN improves markers of metabolic health, which is itself relevant to fertility
  • Observational links between higher NAD+ status and better ovarian reserve markers
  • Several fertility clinics in Japan and the US now using NAD+ precursors as adjunct support, based on the animal data

So the mechanism is plausible and the animal data is strong, but human reproductive outcomes have not been directly tested yet. Anyone selling NMN as a fertility treatment is ahead of the science.

Dosage and timing for reproductive support

There is no fertility-specific human dosing data, so the sensible approach is the standard studied range: 250 to 500mg per day, taken in the morning. Egg maturation takes roughly 90 days, so researchers studying oocyte quality generally look at interventions over a 2 to 3 month window. If NMN is going to help, short-term use days before an IVF cycle is unlikely to do much.

Who this research is most relevant for

  • Women over 35 planning pregnancy: age-related NAD+ decline in ovarian tissue is the mechanism the animal studies target
  • Couples preparing for IVF: some clinics use NAD+ precursors during the 90-day egg maturation window before retrieval
  • Men with sperm quality concerns: early animal data on motility and oxidative stress, though weaker than the female-side research
  • Anyone told they have diminished ovarian reserve: discuss with your fertility specialist first; NMN is an adjunct, not a treatment
✓ Key Takeaways
  • Egg cells depend heavily on NAD+ for mitochondrial energy and chromosome handling
  • NMN restored egg quality and live birth rates in aged mice (Cell Reports, 2020)
  • No human trial has yet tested NMN on pregnancy outcomes
  • If used, the 90-day egg maturation window is the relevant timeframe
  • Talk to a fertility specialist before adding NMN to any treatment plan
