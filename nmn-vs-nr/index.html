NMN vs NR: Which NAD+ Precursor is Better?

Both NMN and NR raise NAD+ levels — but they differ in cost, absorption, and what the science actually shows. Here's the honest breakdown.

NMN (Nicotinamide Mononucleotide) and NR (Nicotinamide Riboside) are both NAD+ precursors — supplements designed to raise your cellular levels of NAD+, the coenzyme that declines with age and underpins energy production, DNA repair, and metabolic health.

They're often marketed as alternatives to each other, and the debate between them is one of the most common questions in the longevity supplement space. Here's what the science actually says.

The Basics: How They Differ

Both NMN and NR are forms of Vitamin B3. They sit at different points in the NAD+ biosynthesis pathway:

  • NR (Nicotinamide Riboside) → converts to NMN → then converts to NAD+
  • NMN (Nicotinamide Mononucleotide) → converts directly to NAD+

NMN is therefore one step closer to NAD+ than NR. This matters because every conversion step introduces potential inefficiency — enzymes required for conversion may be rate-limited, particularly in older or metabolically stressed tissue.

What the Clinical Trials Say

NMN Human Trials

NMN has accumulated substantial human clinical evidence. Key studies include:

  • Irie et al. (2020) — First human safety trial (100–500mg/day), confirmed NMN safely raises NAD+ in blood and is well-tolerated
  • Yoshino et al. (2021, Science) — 250mg/day NMN improved muscle insulin sensitivity in postmenopausal women with prediabetes — the first human metabolic benefit data
  • Huang et al. (2022) — 600mg/day NMN showed improved aerobic capacity and muscle function in recreational runners aged 40–65
  • Kim et al. (2022) — NMN raised NAD+ in older adults and improved fatigue scores over 12 weeks

NR Human Trials

NR has solid clinical evidence too, with a longer track record (it was studied earlier):

  • Trammell et al. (2016) — NR safely raised NAD+ in blood, the landmark first human safety study
  • Martens et al. (2018) — 1,000mg/day NR raised NAD+ metabolites but showed no significant cardiovascular benefit in healthy older adults
  • Elhassan et al. (2019) — 1,000mg/day NR raised muscle NAD+ in older men, but didn't improve physical performance markers

🔬 Key Takeaway: NMN Shows More Functional Benefits

Both raise NAD+ in blood. But human trials for NMN have more consistently shown downstream functional improvements — muscle function, insulin sensitivity, aerobic capacity. NR trials have largely confirmed NAD+ elevation without consistently translating this into measurable clinical outcomes. This gap is likely to narrow as more NR trials complete, but as of 2026, NMN has the more compelling functional evidence base.

The Absorption Question

One of the longest-running debates was whether NMN could even be absorbed intact, or whether it had to be broken down to NR first (then re-converted). A 2019 study by Grozio et al. identified a specific NMN transporter — SLC12A8 — in the gut lining, providing evidence that NMN can be absorbed directly into cells without first being converted to NR.

This was significant because it means NMN may bypass the NR→NMN conversion step entirely in some tissues, potentially making it more efficient than the biosynthesis pathway would suggest.

NR, by contrast, is absorbed through a combination of passive diffusion and transporter-mediated uptake. It's well-absorbed, but relies on conversion to NMN inside cells before becoming NAD+.

Cost Comparison

NR has historically been cheaper because it's been commercially available longer and manufacturing is more established. NMN has come down significantly in price over the past few years as production has scaled, but quality NMN still typically costs slightly more per month than comparable NR doses.

However: NMN trials have used doses of 250–600mg/day, while NR trials frequently required 1,000mg/day or more to show meaningful NAD+ elevation. When you account for effective dose, the cost difference narrows considerably or disappears entirely.
